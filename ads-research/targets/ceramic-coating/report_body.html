Read this first: what the data cannot tell you

Meta publishes no spend, impressions or conversions for commercial ads. Two signals survive: how long an ad has been live, and where the advertiser is putting new money. Everything below ranks on those. Longevity means an ad was not killed — it does not mean profitable.

Dead-ad tenure is censored. Foreplay reports roughly one day for almost every inactive ad regardless of how long it really ran. That is a data artifact, not a stop date. So every day-figure on this page is computed from live ads only; dead ads contribute to counts and nothing else. This is a single snapshot — a state, not a trend. A second pull in two weeks would give real kill dates for everything live today.

The discovery index is not a ranking tool. A text search for "ceramic coating" returned TikTok organic clips with running_duration: null, duplicate records, and a non-stick cookware ad. Searching "paint protection film" returned German film production companies. Every number here comes from per-page pulls instead. The brand index also overstates library size by roughly half — it claimed 1,389 ads for one advertiser that returned 195.

The offer, dismantled

This is the part that transfers. The service segment converges on one structure, and it is not "cheap ceramic coating".

1. The biggest number in the ad is never the price

{{N_50OFF}} of the {{N_ANYOFF}} service ads that name any discount use exactly 50% off. Not 20, not 25. But the percentage is almost never said alone — it is converted into absolute dollars, and when you classify every dollar figure by the words around it, the two most-repeated numbers in the category are a saving and the value of the free add-ons. The actual price comes third.

2. Never discount further — add a free adjacent service

Once the price is halved it stays put. The extra value is always a different service with its own stated dollar value, so the core price is never eroded.

3. Gate the bonus behind the watch-time

The most-copied line in the corpus reveals the bonus as a reward for finishing the video: "since you watched the end of this video, we're throwing in a free windshield ceramic coating." It does double duty — it lifts completion rate and it makes the bonus feel earned rather than discounted.

Retargeting: the hole in the category

You asked for retargeting examples. The honest answer is that there are almost none to show, and the reason matters.

Ad libraries never publish audience targeting. Nothing in the data says "this ad was shown to a warm audience". So retargeting can only be inferred two ways, and I checked both:

  • Format. A dynamic product ad (dpa) is retargeting by construction. There are {{N_DPA}} in the whole corpus — and {{N_DPA_SVC}} in the service segment.
  • Copy addressed to someone who already engaged — "still thinking about it", "your quote", "you came back". Every hit was a false positive. The phrase that kept matching, "since you watched the end of this video", is a bonus reveal aimed at a cold viewer, not a warm-audience call-out.

So: the ceramic-coating service category runs essentially all cold, all on-platform, all top-of-funnel. The retargeting layer that a $995–$2,295 considered purchase would obviously justify is simply not being run by the advertisers in this scan. Treat that as the opening, not as a reason to skip it.

What I'd take from this

  1. Copy the offer architecture, not the price. Anchor, halve, state the saving in dollars, then add a free adjacent service with its own value. Never discount twice.
  2. Put the city in the first line. It is what {{TOP_HOOK_N}} of the last 60 days' launches do.
  3. Gate the bonus behind watch-time. One line, two jobs.
  4. Build the retargeting layer nobody is running. {{N_DPA_SVC}} dynamic ads in {{N_SVC}} service ads is a category-wide blind spot on a four-figure considered purchase.
  5. Test a capture page against the instant form. {{PCT_ONPLAT}}% of the category stays on Meta. That is consensus, and consensus is untested by definition.
